# Capstone Research Guide

## Scope boundary declaration

This is project-authored, general guidance for research-based capstone work. It is not an official institutional guide, assessment rubric, or substitute for current instructions from a course or supervisor.

It covers broadly useful practices for framing a project, reviewing public literature, describing methods, keeping evidence traceable, reporting progress, discussing limitations, and presenting conclusions that match the available evidence.

It excludes institution-specific rules, grading criteria, schedules, forms, names, student or group identifiers, contact details, branded materials, screenshots, and copied passages from restricted sources.

## Suggested workflow

1. **Define the project.** State the problem, intended outcome, constraints, and boundaries. Turn the topic into a question that can be addressed with the time and resources available.
2. **Review public sources.** Keep a search log, record bibliographic details, and explain how each source relates to the project question. Separate published findings from the project's own observations.
3. **Describe the method.** Explain what material or data will be used, how it will be handled, and how the analysis or implementation will be carried out. Record permissions and privacy constraints where they apply.
4. **Keep an evidence trail.** Version project files and record the inputs, configuration, commands, and outputs needed to understand or reproduce important results. Do not report a result without pointing to its supporting evidence.
5. **Evaluate against stated goals.** Explain what was measured, what the result supports, and what remains unknown. Keep proposed targets separate from measured outcomes.
6. **Report progress clearly.** Summarize completed work, evidence produced, current blockers, risks, and the next planned steps. Link claims to the relevant source or project artifact.
7. **Prepare a demonstration.** Show the problem, the approach, representative evidence, limitations, and what the audience should take away. Make clear which parts are implemented and which remain proposals.

## Evidence and reporting

For each important statement, make its basis traceable. Distinguish among:

- findings reported by public sources;
- observations produced by the project;
- design choices or future work proposed by the project.

Use precise terms for measured quantities, describe the conditions under which results were obtained, and state limitations beside the conclusions they qualify. Avoid implying that a design target has been achieved before supporting evidence exists.

## Public release check

Before publishing a project document, review the text, tables, figures, speaker notes, comments, links, and file metadata. Remove identifying details and institution-specific requirements that are not approved for public release. Rewrite the explanation in original, general terms instead of copying passages or templates from restricted materials. Confirm that every public link points to an approved public source.

This guide contains general research and project-management practices only. For institution-specific requirements, use the current official materials through the appropriate private channel.
